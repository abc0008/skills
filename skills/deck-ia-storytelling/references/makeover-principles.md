# SWD makeover-library principles

Source: the *storytelling with data* chart-makeover library
(storytellingwithdata.com/makeovers) — 13 makeovers, each distilled to a check + fix.
Use these as concrete, tactical checks layered on top of the main rubric.

## 1. Make the point clear *(is-your-point-clear)*
- **Lesson:** if you don't state the purpose, the insight gets lost or the audience
  reaches the wrong conclusion. Spell out the takeaway in a **takeaway-sentence slide
  title**; add an explicit **recommended action** ("so what?") as text on the slide.
  Words + pictures supporting each other boosts understanding *and recall*.
- **Check:** is the takeaway written out? Is an action stated? **Fix:** add a pithy
  takeaway title at top + a recommendation line, then iterate the chart to reinforce
  them (aggregate where individual detail isn't needed; drop the dual axis).

## 2. Don't hide the crucial findings *(dont-hide-the-crucial-findings)*
- **Lesson:** critical info can be present but not retrievable. Choose a more
  appropriate visual and actively call attention to the key finding.
- **Check:** is the most important number/finding easy to extract at a glance?
  **Fix:** re-chart for retrievability; use an accent + annotation to surface it.

## 3. Tackle scope creep *(diverging-bar / scope-creep)*
- **Lesson:** slides accrete additions until they're impenetrable; nobody intends it.
- **Check:** has the slide accumulated layers/series/notes beyond its original point?
  **Fix:** strip back to the core message; remove accreted elements; rebuild to one
  point.

## 4. Dashboard → story *(from-dashboard-to-story)*
- **Lesson:** dashboards are for *exploration*, not for communicating a specific
  insight. To drive action, build a **separate, focused presentation** rather than
  screenshotting the dashboard. Use the **Big Idea as a filter** to decide which data
  to bring and what to leave behind. Pair words + color; add an active title and an
  explicit recommendation; one-slide executive summary.
- **Check:** is a deck just dashboard dumps competing with filters/unrelated charts?
  **Fix:** isolate only the charts that support the Big Idea; re-present with takeaway
  title, color+words, and a CTA.

## 5. Multi-level makeover *(simplifying-a-shrinkage-report)*
- **Lesson:** improvement happens at multiple levels — a single visual, the overall
  document structure, or both, depending on need and the end use.
- **Check:** does the deck need detail fixes, structural fixes, or both?
  **Fix:** decide scope explicitly; address structure (sectioning/flow) as well as
  individual charts.

## 6. Arrange intuitively, emphasize thoughtfully *(bar-charts-and-dot-plots)*
- **Lesson:** reduce interpretation effort by understanding context, ordering data
  intuitively (e.g., sort by value), and emphasizing the key data deliberately.
- **Check:** is the data ordered to aid reading (not alphabetical-by-default)? Is
  emphasis intentional? **Fix:** sort meaningfully; add one accent to the key series.

## 7. Graph beats table for a message *(visualizing-gender-identity)*
- **Lesson:** tables suit value lookup or required specificity; if there's an
  overarching message, graph it. (A square-area chart beat a survey table here.)
- **Check:** is a table being used where the point is a pattern? **Fix:** convert to a
  graph that shows the pattern.

## 8. Don't pack everything into one graph *(numbers-of-different-magnitudes)*
- **Lesson:** answering too many questions in one visual answers none well. Use
  **multiple focused graphs**, tie text visually to the data, and build distinct
  versions for live presentation vs. leave-behind.
- **Check:** is one chart trying to do several jobs (and mixing magnitudes/axes)?
  **Fix:** split into several single-purpose graphs.

## 9. Insight vs. call to action *(a-story-about-opportunity)*
- **Lesson:** ask whether the data is a *success* to report or a *call to action* —
  reframing reveals new ways to visualize that lead to better insight. Move from
  "showing data" to "answering a question."
- **Check:** does the slide merely show a takeaway, or does it answer the audience's
  question and prompt action? **Fix:** reframe around the question + the action.

## 10. Design with the audience's end-goal in mind *(lets-improve-this-graph)*
- **Lesson:** before showing data, ask *why am I communicating with data at all?* Five
  audience-centered questions sharpen the communication.
- **Check:** is the design driven by the audience's goal or by the data you happen to
  have? **Fix:** restate the audience goal and redesign toward it.

## 11. Trends → line graphs *(a-heatmap-makeover)*
- **Lesson:** time-series trends are easier to spot in line graphs than tables (or
  heatmaps); audience needs dictate further choices.
- **Check:** is time-series data shown as a table/heatmap/bars? **Fix:** use a line
  graph for the trend.

## 12. Short on time? Prioritize audience, words, graph choice *(quick-presentation-makeover)*
- **Lesson:** under time pressure, you can still plan + create + deliver in ~1 hour by
  prioritizing three things: **audience, words, and graph choice.**
- **Check:** if time-boxed, are those three locked before polishing? **Fix:** nail
  audience + takeaway words + the right chart first; skip cosmetic polish.

## 13. Connect the dots — show relationships *(connecting-the-dots)*
- **Lesson:** when data looks disparate but isn't, design so the audience doesn't have
  to work to see the link: effective visuals, **active titles**, and **strategic
  color** make associations apparent.
- **Check:** are related pieces of data visually connected? **Fix:** use active titles
  + shared color to make the relationships explicit.

## 14. Use familiar, easy-to-read graphs *(an-alternative-to-treemaps)*
- **Lesson:** a handful of recognizable charts get the point across; sticking to
  familiar visuals lets audiences decipher, understand, *and act* faster. (A simple bar
  chart beat a treemap.)
- **Check:** is a novelty/complex chart used where a standard bar/line would read
  faster? **Fix:** swap to the familiar standard chart.

## Cross-cutting themes (recurring across the library)
- **Explanatory ≠ exploratory** — isolate the pearls; leave the rest behind.
- **Active/takeaway titles** beat topic-label titles on nearly every makeover.
- **Words + color, color-matched to the data**, is the most repeated single tactic.
- **Right chart > clever chart**; familiar and easy-to-read wins.
- **Order and emphasis are decisions**, not defaults.
- **Always end on an action / so-what.**
