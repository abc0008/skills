The bottom line

The workflows that held up were built around a specific question, used definitions finance had already agreed, showed evidence a reviewer could check, were judged by the work they left for the team, and were tested when rules changed. In all five, the AI prepares the case and a named person makes the decision. The article offers these as directional guidance and reports no time-saved or ROI figures.

Lesson 1

Start from the business question, not the data that happens to match

A specific question tells the workflow which evidence it needs for the decision.

InvoicePurchase orderOutdated rate on both

The question: are suppliers charging the negotiated price?

Each invoice lineContract termReview queueBuyer decides

What to notice: the invoice-to-PO match was not wrong, it answered a different question. Tying each line to the contract is what surfaces the discrepancy, and the decision to pursue it stays with the buyer.

The case behind it

A distributor's procurement team wanted to know whether suppliers were charging negotiated prices. Invoices matched purchase orders, but an outdated rate could appear on both and pass. The workflow linked each invoice line to its contract terms; the review queue showed a recalculated amount and the supporting term for each possible discrepancy. Buyers checked the flags and prepared questions for suppliers.

Lesson 2

Give AI the definitions finance has agreed, or it will reproduce the disagreement

Finance work leaves almost no room for error, so the agent needs the team's own rules for what counts and how shared costs are split.

Before: each workbook decidesAfter: finance decides once
Shared costsAllocated differently in each workbookOne approved split, agreed by finance
Customer identitySales and finance use different identifiersReconciled as part of the agreed rules
The calculationRebuilt by hand; data in separate systemsAgent applies the approved rules; repeatable and explainable
When a number looks offHard to say which assumption caused itTraced to its inputs, corrected, rerun

What to notice: the agent did not invent a definition of customer acquisition cost. Finance agreed one, and the agent's job was to apply it the same way every time and show its working.

The case behind it

A software company's finance team was comparing customer acquisition cost across products. Workbooks allocated shared costs differently, the data sat in separate systems, and sales and finance used different customer identifiers. Finance agreed which costs belonged in the comparison and how to split shared costs, then gave those rules to the agent. In review, finance traced an unexpected difference to a shared-cost assumption, corrected it and reran the comparison.

Lesson 3

An approval needs evidence and a named approver. "Be careful" is not a control.

Reviewers need to see which constraints a recommendation meets and why the alternatives were rejected.

The alternative

Lower-cost funding optionWhy each asset qualified or failed

The recommendation

Scenarios + remaining capacityTreasury reviewNamed approverAuthorized staff

What to notice: three separate human roles follow the AI step: review, approval and execution. The workflow names who holds each one instead of relying on a cautious prompt.

The case behind it

In a treasury review, the team needed to understand why a lower-cost funding option had been excluded. Analysts had been combining asset data with agreements, eligibility rules and facility limits by hand. The workflow prepared scenarios with reviewed calculations, remaining facility capacity and an explanation of why each asset qualified or failed, so treasury could trace exclusions, check feasible alternatives and route the recommendation for approval.

Lesson 4

Judge a workflow by the work it leaves behind, not the output it produces

The value is in how much checking, correction and follow-up remains for the team.

What changed in the reconciliation

Same proposed matchesSmaller exception queue

What still has to be measured

CheckingCorrectingFollow-upCost to runTrue cost

What to notice: matching on amounts alone produced plenty of output and sent accountants back into the records, including for cases they had already closed. The improvement was a shorter, better-supported queue. The article reports no time-saved figure; the team says that measurement is still to do.

The case behind it

In bank reconciliation for a multi-entity services business, too many proposed matches needed rechecking. Amount-only matching missed timing differences and payments that covered several entries. The team grouped related ledger entries, attached supporting evidence, and separated routine timing differences from unexplained discrepancies, so accepted matches could be traced to source records. Testing with accountants showed which steps still required investigation.

Lesson 5

Test a rule change on the cases it should move and the ones it should leave alone

A fix is only safe when the right decisions change and unrelated ones stay the same.

Should change

Updated allowance: one client, one dateEligible requestsDecision changes

Should not change

Other clients' policiesTheir requestsDecision unchanged

What to notice: the rerun checks both rows. Versioned rules, with each recommendation linked to its policy evidence, are what make it possible to change one client's allowance without touching anyone else's.

The case behind it

A services company handled expenses and supplier invoices under client-specific policies, amendments and exceptions. A reviewer caught an expense rejection based on an outdated allowance that applied to one client and one effective date. The team had converted approved guidance into versioned rules, so the rejection could be traced to its source and the right version updated. They then reran previously reviewed cases: the amendment applied to eligible requests and other clients stayed under their own policies.
