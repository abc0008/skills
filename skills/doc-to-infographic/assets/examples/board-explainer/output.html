The M&A target diligence framework, at a glance

How a commercial-bank acquisition is taken from first look to a bid decision — the phases, the people, the FP&A spine that carries it, and the two real go/no-go calls along the way.

The bottom line

The framework exists to answer one question: should we acquire this target, at this price and terms? It runs a disciplined diligence process across six weekly phases and eight working groups, converging on a recommendation across five decision dimensions — with Finance / FP&A as the economic spine that runs through every phase, turning each specialist team's findings into normalized earnings, revenue durability, and workforce durability.

Diligence moves through six phases — and only two of them are real go/no-go calls

What to notice: the whole process is one sequence — the decisions are not a separate exercise. Most gates simply sharpen the next round of work; only the two diamonds (target assessment and the decision package) can send the deal toward continue, reprice, or no-bid.

How the source maps to these phases

The framework's illustrative timeline runs Week 0 (VDR access, kick-off) through Week 5 (resolve final-round items, LOI/bid support). Phases 1 and 3 correspond to the two natural decision points: the early target-assessment / strategic-fit screen, and the final decision-ready synthesis where findings become a bid recommendation.

FP&A is the economic spine — five workstreams run the length of the deal, and every specialist finding plugs into them

The finance workstream is not one of the parallel teams; it is the through-line. Its five workstreams move left to right with the deal, and the specialist groups' findings — credit, deposits, regulatory, workforce, technology — feed into the band, where they convert into the numbers that drive the decision.

Five decision dimensions all feed one call — none of them stands alone

A decision-ready memo has to clear all five. The diagram shows them converging on the single recommendation, not sitting as separate scorecards.

What to notice: the five arrows land on a single node — the framework is explicit that the recommendation must be clear "across five dimensions," so a strong score on one (say strategic fit) cannot rescue a weak read on another (say workforce durability).
