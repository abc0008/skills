# Commercial Banking M&A Target Evaluation & Due Diligence Framework

## 1. Purpose

This document outlines a structured framework for evaluating a potential M&A target in a U.S. commercial banking acquisition process. It combines a typical investment banking-style target deck structure, virtual data room diligence workflow, working group organization, due diligence memo template, and specific analytical workstreams focused on revenue durability, workforce durability, target-level financials, and segment-level profitability.

The framework is designed for an internal corporate development, finance, strategy, or FP&A team supporting evaluation of a potential acquisition target.

---

## 2. Core Evaluation Question

The acquirer’s diligence team is ultimately trying to answer:

> Should we acquire this target, at this price, under these terms, given the strategic rationale, financial return profile, operational complexity, regulatory considerations, integration requirements, and key risks?

For a commercial banking acquirer, the core diligence themes typically include:

- Strategic fit
- Financial performance and normalized earnings power
- Revenue durability
- Workforce durability
- Credit and risk profile
- Deposit and funding quality
- Regulatory and compliance considerations
- Cost and revenue synergy potential
- Integration complexity and expected costs
- Valuation support
- Final-round outstanding diligence items

## 3. Typical M&A Target Deck Structure

A structured target evaluation deck should generally include the following sections.

### 3.1 Executive Summary

- Target name and business overview
- Transaction context
- Summary recommendation
- Key strategic rationale
- Preliminary valuation view
- Key benefits
- Key considerations and risks
- Required next steps

### 3.2 Strategic Rationale

#### Benefits and Considerations

Summarize the primary strategic benefits of the transaction, along with the major considerations that need to be evaluated before proceeding.

Potential benefits may include:

- Expanded presence in attractive markets
- Access to new customer segments
- Improved scale in existing geographies
- Enhanced product capabilities
- Revenue diversification
- Increased fee income contribution
- Stronger relationship coverage
- Cross-sell opportunities
- Operating leverage and cost synergy potential
- Improved long-term growth profile

Potential considerations may include:

- Revenue concentration risk
- Key employee retention risk
- Integration complexity
- Cultural fit
- Technology conversion risk
- Regulatory and compliance issues
- Credit or portfolio risk
- Customer attrition risk
- Purchase price and tangible book value earnback
- Execution risk around cost saves and revenue synergies

### 3.3 Target Market Size

Provide a view of the relevant addressable market, including:

- Geographic footprint
- Market size and growth rate
- Deposit market share, if relevant
- Loan market opportunity
- Fee income opportunity
- Competitive landscape
- Demographic and economic trends
- Commercial banking opportunity by segment
- Relevant industry verticals

### 3.4 Summary of Existing Relationship

If the acquirer has an existing relationship with the target, summarize:

- Prior discussions or partnership history
- Existing customer, vendor, lending, treasury, wealth, or capital markets relationships
- Known leadership relationships
- Prior transaction discussions, if any
- Existing data access or institutional knowledge
- Relationship strengths or concerns

### 3.5 Strategic Merits

Evaluate the target against the acquirer’s strategic priorities:

- Does the target deepen or expand priority markets?
- Does it add attractive customer segments?
- Does it improve scale or operating leverage?
- Does it add capabilities the acquirer lacks?
- Does it strengthen the acquirer’s competitive positioning?
- Does it support long-term growth priorities?
- Does the transaction improve franchise quality?

### 3.6 Overview of Overlapping Capabilities

Identify where the target and acquirer overlap across:

- Products
- Geographies
- Customer segments
- Relationship managers
- Branches or physical presence
- Technology platforms
- Operations functions
- Risk and compliance functions
- Finance, HR, legal, audit, and corporate infrastructure
- Wholesale, commercial, retail, wealth, or specialty lines of business

This section should help determine where the transaction creates scale, redundancy, integration requirements, or synergy potential.

## 5. Working Group Structure

A disciplined diligence process should begin by defining internal working groups and assigning clear ownership.

### 5.1 Core Working Groups

Typical working groups include:

| Working Group | Primary Diligence Focus |
|---|---|
| Accounting | Historical financials, purchase accounting, one-time items, accounting policy alignment |
| Audit | Internal controls, audit findings, SOX implications, control environment |
| Legal | Transaction structure, legal diligence, contracts, litigation, governance, LOI and definitive agreement support |
| Finance | Financial model, valuation, segment financials, synergies, normalized earnings, management reporting |
| Human Resources | Employee population, compensation, benefits, retention, severance, incentive plans, cultural fit |
| Risk and Credit | Credit quality, portfolio composition, criticized/classified assets, risk ratings, allowance, underwriting standards |
| Technology and Operations | Core systems, data conversion, cyber, business process optimization, operational integration |
| Relevant LOB Leaders | Commercial, wholesale, wealth, retail, specialty, or product-specific diligence and business-line integration |

### 5.2 Example Focus Team Structure

Example focus teams may include:

- Accounting
- Audit
- Finance
- Human Resources
- Legal
- Risk and Credit
- Technology, Operations, and Security
- Wholesale Banking or other relevant line-of-business leaders

### 5.3 Working Group Responsibilities

Each working group should be responsible for:

- Reviewing applicable VDR materials
- Identifying missing information
- Submitting document requests
- Submitting key diligence questions
- Attending working group meetings
- Preparing diligence findings
- Quantifying financial, operational, or integration impacts
- Identifying risks and opportunities
- Supporting the due diligence memo
- Escalating unresolved items for final-round diligence

## 8. Finance and Strategic Analysis Workstreams

The finance and strategic finance diligence workstreams should harden the acquirer’s view of the target’s revenue durability, workforce durability, normalized earnings power, and segment-level profitability.

### 8.1 Revenue Durability Analysis

#### Customer Revenue NLE: New, Lost, Existing

Analyze customer revenue across three categories:

| Category | Definition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| New | Revenue from customers added during the measurement period | Measures new business generation |
| Lost | Revenue from customers that churned or materially declined | Identifies revenue leakage and customer attrition |
| Existing | Revenue retained from continuing customers | Measures recurring revenue durability |

Key questions:

- What portion of revenue is recurring versus dependent on new customer acquisition?
- How much revenue has been lost from prior-period customers?
- Are new customers replacing lost revenue at acceptable margins?
- Are revenue trends broad-based or concentrated in a small number of customers?
- Is revenue growth driven by durable customer relationships or one-time activity?

Potential outputs:

- Revenue bridge by customer cohort
- New/lost/existing revenue waterfall
- Customer concentration table
- Segment-level revenue retention
- Revenue by relationship manager or team
- Recurring versus non-recurring revenue classification

### 8.2 Employee Distribution Analysis

Evaluate the target’s employee base by:

- Business line
- Function
- Geography
- Team
- Role
- Seniority
- Revenue-producing versus support roles
- Critical employee status
- Compensation level
- Incentive eligibility

Key questions:

- Where is the workforce concentrated?
- Which teams generate the most revenue?
- Which roles are critical for revenue retention?
- Where are there duplicative roles?
- Where are there potential integration or retention risks?

### 8.3 Employee NLE: New, Lost, Existing

Analyze workforce movement across new, lost, and existing employees.

| Category | Definition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| New | Employees hired during the period | Measures hiring velocity and expansion areas |
| Lost | Employees who departed during the period | Measures attrition and potential instability |
| Existing | Employees retained across periods | Measures workforce continuity |

Key questions:

- Is the workforce stable?
- Are key revenue producers leaving?
- Are new hires concentrated in strategic or non-strategic areas?
- Is employee attrition creating customer or operational risk?
- Are key teams durable enough to support the acquisition thesis?

### 8.4 Compensation vs. Revenue Analysis

Evaluate the relationship between compensation expense and revenue production.

Potential analyses:

- Compensation as a percentage of revenue by segment
- Compensation as a percentage of revenue by team
- Revenue per employee
- Revenue per revenue-producing employee
- Incentive compensation as a percentage of revenue
- Fixed versus variable compensation mix
- Compensation growth versus revenue growth
- Margin contribution by team or segment

Key questions:

- Are compensation levels aligned with revenue contribution?
- Are certain teams overcompensated relative to production?
- Are incentives driving desired behavior?
- Are compensation structures sustainable post-close?
- What retention or incentive adjustments may be required?

### 8.5 Incentives by Role and Area

Analyze incentive structures by:

- Role
- Team
- Business line
- Geography
- Revenue type
- Production goals
- Retention importance
- Customer relationship ownership

Key diligence questions:

- Which incentive plans are most important to revenue retention?
- Which employees require retention arrangements?
- Are incentive plans compatible with the acquirer’s compensation philosophy?
- Will incentive changes create attrition or revenue leakage?
- Are there change-in-control or severance costs that need to be modeled?

### 8.6 Revenue and Workforce Durability Summary

The combined objective of the revenue and workforce analyses is to assess:

> How durable is the target’s revenue, and how dependent is that revenue on specific people, teams, customers, or incentive structures?

Key conclusions should inform:

- Valuation
- Retention planning
- Incentive design
- Integration strategy
- Revenue synergy assumptions
- Revenue dis-synergy assumptions
- Management presentation questions
- Final-round diligence requests

## 9. Target Segment-Level Financials

### 9.1 Objective

Develop a segment-level financial view of the target that reflects the core operating profile of each business segment.

This analysis supports:

- Valuation
- Strategic fit assessment
- Segment profitability analysis
- Synergy evaluation
- Management reporting integration
- Transaction model assumptions
- Final recommendation

### 9.2 Transaction-Level Income Attribution

A key analytical workstream is evaluating transaction-level income and attributing it by team or segment.

Potential attribution dimensions:

- Business segment
- Team
- Relationship manager
- Product
- Customer
- Geography
- Revenue type
- Fee category
- Line of business

Key questions:

- Which teams are generating revenue?
- Which segments have the strongest recurring earnings profile?
- Are high-revenue segments also high-margin segments?
- Are revenues properly aligned to the teams that manage the relationships?
- Are there revenue streams that are not clearly attributable?

### 9.3 Core Operating Financial Profile by Segment

Build a normalized segment-level operating profile by identifying and adjusting for one-time items.

#### Adjustments to Consider

| Adjustment Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| One-time revenue | Unusual transaction income, episodic fees, non-recurring gains |
| One-time expense | Legal costs, consulting costs, transaction costs, unusual severance, restructuring costs |
| Run-rate compensation | Adjusted salary, incentive, and benefits expense |
| Allocated overhead | Corporate support, technology, operations, finance, HR, legal, audit |
| Revenue reclassification | Revenue attributed to correct team, product, or segment |
| Expense reclassification | Expense mapped to appropriate business line or support function |

## 11. Risk and Opportunity Framework

### 11.1 Revenue Durability Risks

- Revenue concentrated in a small number of customers
- Revenue concentrated in a small number of employees
- High lost-customer revenue trend
- High one-time revenue contribution
- Weak recurring revenue base
- Customer relationship ownership unclear
- Revenue attribution data incomplete

### 11.2 Workforce Durability Risks

- High employee attrition
- Key producer flight risk
- Compensation mismatch
- Incentive plan disruption
- Weak bench strength
- Cultural mismatch
- Integration-related morale risk

### 11.3 Financial Profile Risks

- One-time revenues overstating run-rate earnings
- One-time expenses obscuring normalized profitability
- Segment margins not well understood
- Incomplete expense allocation
- Unclear team-level profitability
- Overstated synergy assumptions

### 11.4 Key Opportunities

- Improve segment profitability through better cost allocation
- Retain and grow high-value customer relationships
- Align incentives to revenue retention and growth
- Cross-sell acquirer products to target customers
- Improve management reporting by segment
- Rationalize overlapping corporate infrastructure
- Improve operating leverage
- Reallocate resources toward higher-return teams or segments

## 13. Practical Positioning of Finance Work Performed

The internal finance workstream contributed to the diligence process by hardening the analysis around two central questions:

1. How durable is the target’s revenue?
2. How durable is the target’s workforce?

Specific work performed included:

- Customer revenue NLE analysis: new, lost, and existing revenue
- Employee distribution analysis
- Employee NLE analysis: new, lost, and existing employees
- Compensation versus revenue analysis
- Incentive analysis by role and area
- Development of target segment-level financials
- Evaluation of transaction-level income
- Attribution of income by team
- Identification of one-time expenses and revenues
- Development of a core operating financial profile by segment
- Support for valuation assumptions
- Input into key people incentive structures

This work helped connect the target’s reported financial performance to the underlying drivers of sustainable earnings, revenue retention, employee retention, and segment-level profitability.

## 14. Decision-Ready Summary Framework

A final internal presentation or memo should make the answer clear across five dimensions.

| Dimension | Core Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic Fit | Does the target improve the acquirer’s franchise? | Strategic rationale and market fit |
| Financial Return | Does the deal create acceptable value? | Valuation, EPS, TBV earnback, ROIC, segment financials |
| Revenue Durability | Is revenue sustainable post-close? | Customer NLE, recurring revenue, revenue concentration |
| Workforce Durability | Are the people required to retain revenue likely to stay? | Employee NLE, key people, compensation, incentives |
| Integration Executability | Can the acquirer realize the deal thesis? | Synergy plan, integration cost estimate, open items |
